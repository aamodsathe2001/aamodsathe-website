Over the last few weeks, we’ve explored how the data function is changing—from basic data provisioning to systems orchestration—and the natural evolution of our teams and leadership styles in an AI-first world.

Today, I want to tackle a more fundamental puzzle: **Where you build determines how you build.**

Throughout my career, I’ve operated at opposite ends of the organizational spectrum. I spent years directing data and ML strategies at some of the world's largest, most resource-rich tech giants, and today I lead the unified data and analytics function at a fast-moving, hyper-growth startup.

The differences between these two worlds are often oversimplified as "big vs. small" or "slow vs. fast." But in the AI era, the contrast runs far deeper. It is about how data actually works under different gravity fields, how you manage risk, and what AI does to the economics of decision-making.

If you are trying to adapt your data strategy to a new scale, here is the ground-level reality of what happens when the playbooks collide.

### The Gravity of Big Tech: Moving an Empire

In massive, established companies, the data landscape is vast, complex, and highly guarded. You aren’t just building a pipeline; you are managing a massive ecosystem.

There is an undeniable advantage to this scale: when you move, and if your move is correct, the scale of impact is enormous. At this level, you truly "go slow to go fast." You spend months aligning cross-functional teams, getting varying opinions, and ensuring massive infrastructure is bulletproof because the scale works both on the upside and the downside. A minor tweak to a recommendation model or an ad interface can generate billions in incremental value—or it can degrade the user experience for hundreds of millions of people.

However, the biggest issue with large organizations is that they can sometimes over-index on the dangers of moving and heavily underestimate the speed of output.

When you have thousands of specialized roles and strict guardrails, it is easy to get caught in a state of analysis paralysis. In the age of AI, this "wait and see" posture has become the ultimate danger. While big companies worry about the potential risks of deploying an AI-native feature or changing a legacy model, they often fail to realize that stagnation is the biggest hazard of all.

At this scale, the impact of falling behind isn’t immediately apparent. It is the proverbial frog in a slow-boiling pot. By the time the temperature is visibly rising, the cultural and technical lag can be nearly impossible to reverse.

### The Hyper-Growth Reality: Rebuilding at 10x Speed

At a fast-growing, hyper-growth startup, you have no such luxury—and honestly, I am incredibly grateful for that.

When you don’t have unlimited resources, everything is on the table. Every legacy architectural decision must be revisited, challenged, and often rewritten on the fly.

Recently at Vanta , we faced the challenge of entirely rebuilding our analytics infrastructure to focus on self-serve data. In big tech, a project of this scale would take multiple quarters, dozens of review meetings, and millions in budget. We did it in record time with minimal spend, leveraging AI as a key driver.

We moved that fast because of two things: **uncompromising speed of action and complete team autonomy.** When the distance between a critical decision and execution is minutes rather than months, you don't waste time on endless approvals. You test, you fail, you iterate, and you ship.

In a hyper-growth environment, data isn't just a reporting tool; it is the core differentiator that separates you from competitors, allows you to move fast, and wins over customers. But we have to build that differentiation under a massive constraint: **we do not have unlimited tokens.**

We cannot simply throw compute at every problem. This scarcity actually leads to the most intense, creative innovation per workday I have ever observed in my life. We are forced to build clever, hybrid solutions—leveraging expensive frontier models only for tasks they are uniquely suited for, while using homegrown, open-source, or smaller models for the high-volume, repeatable heavy lifting.

In the AI era, the "all-you-can-eat" compute model will rapidly come to an end. AI is the differentiator, but cost of compute per outcome will increasingly separate the winners from the losers. At a startup, you feel that reality on day one, forcing a level of architectural discipline that big-company budgets often mask.

### The Personal Trade-Offs: The Joy of End-to-End Ownership

The differences between these environments ultimately come down to the human experience of the work.

In big tech, you learn how to collaborate at an elite level. You learn how to navigate complex human situations, build consensus across highly diverse teams, and integrate varying opinions to make progress. It is a masterclass in scale, but the natural trade-off is specialization—you own a highly impactful, yet narrow, slice of the puzzle.

At a startup, you have the rare, exhilarating opportunity to own and run something truly end-to-end. You aren't just analyzing the metric; you are building the ingestion pipeline, tweaking the model, designing the workflow, and seeing the direct business impact of your work by lunchtime.

Does this mean one is universally better? Absolutely not. Both have their place, and both will teach you invaluable lessons. But if you want to build at the absolute frontier of what AI can do, you have to recognize that the old rules of scale and safety are being re-evaluated.

In the AI era, the biggest risk is no longer making a move—it is staying exactly where you are.
