Last week, we talked about how the data function is being rewritten—how we are moving from a model of *providing* data to *orchestrating* it, and why this demands that data leaders get closer to the technical details than ever before.

But as we build these highly leveraged, "10x" teams, a much larger question looms: **How do we actually lead the humans who build them?**

Over the last two to three years, I’ve observed an unsettling trend in our industry. In many circles, leadership has shifted toward what I can only call a reign of terror, or a blanket of fear.

It is almost as if you are not considered a "smart," "consequential," or "high-mobility" leader in a top-tier organization unless the people around you are terrified of you. There is an unspoken, toxic assumption that has taken hold: *You are only as smart as the level of discomfort you can inflict on others.*

Don’t get me wrong. Over the last two decades, I’ve met some of the most brilliant minds on the planet. There is a tiny fraction of people—less than one percent—whose pure, blinding intellect can naturally make you hold your breath when you enter a room. That is a kind of awe, and it is usually driven by a mutual obsession with the work.

I’m not talking about that 1%.

I am talking about the other 99%—people who desperately want to be seen as ruthlessly smart, and who believe the only way to manifest that status is by deliberately making everyone around them feel small, anxious, and uncomfortable.

And I, for one, refuse to go with that flow.

### The Slippery Slope of Self-Inflicted Culture

I don’t know exactly when this cutthroat behavior became the celebrated norm in tech, but its acceleration was heavily supercharged after the initial AI wave. For a while, the industry narrative had almost become: *"We don't need people anymore."*

That narrative has been thoroughly debunked. Now that we have all spent some real time using and building with AI, we know the truth: AI is a massive leverage tool, but the most critical bottlenecks—system design, data trust, organizational alignment, and actual product judgment—are still entirely human.

When leaders choose to go down the slippery slope of fear-based management, they destroy the natural fabric of a team. Here’s the hard truth: **There are few things harder to fix in business than culture—especially a culture that was self-inflicted.**

Once fear becomes the operating system, the damage is almost irreversible:

- 
**The death of truth:** When people are afraid, they stop bringing you bad news. They hide the broken pipelines, the flawed models, and the bad data.

- 
**The collapse of innovation:** In a fear-based environment, no one takes risks. People play it safe to avoid becoming the target, which is the death knell for any team trying to operate at the cutting edge of AI.

- 
**The talent drain:** High-caliber talent has options. The best people do not stay where they are merely tolerated or terrorized; they migrate to environments built on trust and autonomy.

### The Better Path: Servant Leadership with a High Bar

I believe deeply in a servant leadership style. This doesn’t mean being soft, and it certainly doesn’t mean lowering the bar. In fact, you can hold a relentlessly high bar *because* you are kind.

A great leader doesn't need to be the smartest person in the room on every single topic. Instead, they need to know enough of the subject matter to ask the right probing, challenging questions (which we discussed last week) while ensuring the team is constantly pulled toward the right destination.

There is an enormous difference between pushing people down to look big, and pulling people up to make them big. To evaluate your own style, try looking at it through these four shifts:

- 
**Focusing on dominance ➔ Focusing on systemic solutions:** A fear-based leader looks for blame to assert their authority. A kind, high-trust leader digs into system root causes to solve problems together.

- 
**Driving compliance ➔ Driving autonomy:** A fear-based leader uses anxiety to ensure teams do exactly what they are told. A kind leader builds ownership so team members act with real autonomy.

- 
**Proving yourself ➔ Seeking the truth:** A fear-based leader out-talks the room to prove their own intellect. A kind leader asks deep, probing questions to uncover the ground-level truth.

- 
**Sourcing utility ➔ Investing in people:** A fear-based leader sees their team as replaceable execution units. A kind leader knows their people are the ultimate differentiator.

By creating a high-trust, high-accountability culture, you empower your team to operate with true autonomy. You make it safe for them to take on the massive ownership that this new AI era requires—like data scientists writing engineering PRs or deploying full-loop agentic systems.

### How Do We Screen for Kindness?

This shift in tech raises an uncomfortable question: **What is our responsibility when we see this fear-based behavior in an organization—and how do we prevent it from entering our cultures in the first place?**

Screening for a leader’s emotional maturity and genuine kindness is incredibly difficult. Nobody displays toxic, fear-based patterns in an interview; everyone plays the role of the inspiring, empathetic leader when the stakes are high. It is virtually impossible to detect these qualities through traditional interview questions or curated, executive-level references.

That is why, when I am evaluating and hiring leaders for my organizations, I make it a priority to go backchannel: **I actively seek out and talk to the teams they have actually led.**

I want the unfiltered perspective of the individual contributors, the analysts, and the engineers who worked under them day-to-day. How did they feel when things went wrong? Were they empowered, or were they walking on eggshells? That is where the truth lives. If you are hiring leaders to build high-trust, sustainable organizations, you have to be willing to do the hard work of verifying how they treated the people they had authority over.

I don’t have all the answers, and I’m not 100% sure I am entirely right on every nuance. But in my heart and through my years leading teams at Google, Meta, and now Vanta, I am convinced that pulling people up is the only sustainable way to build.

As AI handles more of the manual execution, the uniquely human skills—empathy, cross-functional collaboration, mentorship, and the ability to inspire—will become the ultimate differentiators of successful organizations. Fear is a cheap, short-term motivator. Trust is the only long-term multiplier.
