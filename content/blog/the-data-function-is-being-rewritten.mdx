---
title: "The Data Function Is Being Rewritten"
date: "2026-10-07"
description: "After 20+ years in data, here is what I think is fundamentally changing — and why it matters for data leaders."
---

For more than 20 years, I've watched the data profession evolve.

At eBay, data was becoming a competitive advantage. At PayPal, we were building experimentation and behavioral models to understand consumers. At Google and Meta, data science became deeply embedded in product development, experimentation, ranking, recommendation, and measurement at enormous scale. And now, building agile, AI-first teams at a hyper-growth company like Vanta, I see a stark contrast. Where hyper-scale enterprises rely on thousands of specialized roles and strict domain boundaries, fast-scaling companies demand high velocity, deep cross-functional alignment, and lean operations.

Yet, throughout all of those years and across all those scales, there was one basic assumption about what a data organization did:

We provided data. Sometimes we turned that data into insights. Sometimes those insights influenced a decision. And, if everything went well, the decision eventually led to action.

In other words: Data → Insight → Action

That model served us well. But I think the data function is now being rewritten. And ofcourse AI is the reason.

**From providing data to orchestrating data** If your data team is still primarily focused on "providing data," I think you're already behind the curve. The job is increasingly becoming orchestration.

For as long as I can remember, we've talked about democratizing data. Build the warehouse. Build the semantic layer. Build the dashboards. Build self-service BI. Make it possible for everyone to answer their own questions.

We've made enormous progress, but there was always a bottleneck. Someone still had to know SQL. Someone still had to know the data model. Someone had to find the right tables. Someone had to understand the metrics. Someone had to perform the analysis.

AI changes that equation. We're getting much closer to a world where a product manager, engineer, salesperson, or executive can ask a question in plain English and get a rigorous answer in seconds. At Vanta, I've seen this become much more than a theoretical possibility. We've built an AI-native analytics system that can automatically resolve more than 90% of our internal data questions, with very high accuracy.

That isn't simply a better analytics tool. It represents a fundamentally different operating model. The data team doesn't need to answer every question. The data team needs to build the system that allows the organization to answer its own questions. And that changes what the data team's job is.

**But this doesn't make the data team less important** This is where I think some of the current conversation about AI and data gets it wrong. If everyone can ask an AI a data question, does that mean we no longer need data scientists, analysts, or data engineers? I don't think so. In fact, I think the opposite is true.

Someone still has to define what the data means. Someone has to determine which sources are trustworthy. Someone has to define the metrics. Someone has to establish the right methodology. Someone has to understand how tables should be joined. Someone has to determine whether a statistical conclusion is valid. Someone has to build the architecture through which the AI accesses the data. And, perhaps most importantly, someone has to own whether the answer is actually correct.

That responsibility doesn't disappear because the interface becomes conversational. It becomes more important. The data organization is moving from producing every answer to designing and governing the machinery that produces answers. That's orchestration. And I believe that is one of the biggest changes happening to our profession.

**Data leaders need to get much closer to the technology** There is another change I've become increasingly convinced of. AI is forcing data leaders to become much more technically hands-on.

At large companies, leadership can create some distance from the underlying work. That's partly by necessity. When you're leading organizations of dozens of data scientists and working across product, engineering, and research, you cannot personally do every piece of analysis. You hire great people. You establish standards. You build operating mechanisms. You trust your teams.

I've done that at both Google and Meta, including leading organizations of 60–70+ people and building measurement and experimentation systems at enormous scale.

But AI changes the economics of being hands-on. The cost of doing the work has fallen dramatically. I can write code again. I can build an analysis. I can prototype a system. I can work through an architecture. I can use AI to accelerate the execution by an extraordinary amount.

And I think data leaders increasingly need to do this themselves. Not because leaders should become individual contributors again. But because you cannot effectively lead an AI-native organization if you don't understand the machinery your organization is using.

**The most important leadership skill may be the ability to challenge** This leads to another major shift. Historically, a leader could rely heavily on the expertise of the people around them. If your best data scientist told you that an experiment showed a particular effect, you could ask questions, challenge assumptions, and ultimately trust their expertise.

Now imagine that same analysis was produced in minutes with the help of AI. Should you trust it? Maybe. But you need to know how to determine that.

AI can produce impressive analysis. It can also produce an extremely convincing wrong answer. It can choose the wrong population. It can confuse correlation with causation. It can optimize the wrong metric. It can make an invalid statistical assumption. It can use the wrong data source. It can build something architecturally fragile that looks perfectly reasonable on the surface.

The leadership skill therefore becomes less about knowing every answer and more about knowing how to challenge the answer. What assumption are you making? What would make this conclusion wrong? What's the counterfactual? What is the denominator? What happens if we change the population? What metric are we actually optimizing? What is the failure mode?

Those questions have always mattered. But in an AI-native organization, they become indispensable. Because increasingly, the thing coming to you won't be produced entirely by a human expert. It will be produced by a human directing an increasingly powerful machine. And the leader has to be capable of evaluating the result.

**The boundaries of the data role are disappearing** The third change may be the most exciting. For decades, we've created relatively clean boundaries between data disciplines. A data scientist analyzes. An analyst reports. An engineer builds. A product manager defines the problem. An engineer implements the solution.

AI is collapsing those boundaries. Imagine a data scientist discovering that a particular product behavior is hurting conversion. Historically, the workflow might have been: Analyze → recommend experiment → hand off to Product/Engineering.

Why should it stop there? The data scientist could increasingly: Analyze → formulate experiment → generate the change → prototype it → instrument it → define the statistical framework → create the pull request → hand it to Engineering for review and approval.

I'm not suggesting that every data scientist needs to become a software engineer. I'm suggesting that the scope of what a data professional can own has expanded dramatically.

However, this convergence carries a significant risk. We must be careful that this newfound agility doesn't devolve into unmanaged role blurring causing chaos. Without clear ownership and architectural guardrails, unstructured cross-functional work leads to redundant pipelines, conflicting metrics, and technical debt. To capture the benefits of broad ownership without the chaos, we must establish crisp standards around data governance, code quality, and domain handoffs.

**This changes how we build teams** Once the boundaries of the work change, the structure of the organization has to change too. What should a data scientist know? How much engineering capability should they have? What does an analytics engineer become when AI can generate much of the transformation code? What does a data engineer become when agents can build and maintain significant portions of the pipeline? What does "senior" actually mean when execution itself becomes cheaper? How should career ladders change? How should we define job families? What capabilities should we hire for?

These aren't theoretical questions. They are becoming organizational design questions that every data leader will have to answer. And I don't think the answer is simply to add an "AI Engineer" team next to the existing data organization. The underlying operating model needs to change.

Crucially, as execution itself becomes cheaper, the human side of management becomes our most valuable asset. Scaling these new teams requires embracing the lost art of kind leadership. This means balancing uncompromising high-performance expectations with genuine empathy. It means building high-trust engineering cultures where analysts operate with autonomy and psychological safety. High performance and empathy are not mutually exclusive—they are mutually dependent.

**Are we headed toward 10x data teams?** I believe we are. I'm less certain about the exact timeline. And I don't think simply giving everyone access to an LLM magically makes a team 10x more productive. But I've already seen enough to believe the direction is real.

At Meta, I watched AI-native analytical tooling dramatically reduce analyst cycle time and helped build an environment where AI-driven analytical output went from essentially zero to the majority of analytical output in a relatively short period. At Vanta, we're now pushing that idea further by making large portions of the analytical workflow self-serve and agentic.

These aren't theoretical productivity gains. They are early signals of a much larger change. But getting from 2x to 10x—or eventually beyond—will require more than better models. It will require:

- 
Better data foundations.

- 
Better semantic systems.

- 
Better agents.

- 
Better evaluation.

- 
More technically capable teams.

- 
Different organizational structures.

And perhaps most importantly: A culture willing to change how work gets done. Because I think organizational adoption is going to be the biggest bottleneck. You can build an agent that performs ten steps automatically. But if your organization still requires ten people to manually review every step because nobody trusts the system, you haven't created a 10x organization. You've created a faster tool inside a legacy operating model.

The technology isn't the transformation. The operating model is.

**So what is the job of the data leader now?** After two decades in this field, I'm increasingly thinking about the role in three very different dimensions.

**1. From creating to orchestrating** Don't ask how your team can answer more questions. Ask how you can build a system where the organization can answer most questions itself—while maintaining rigor, trust, and accuracy.

**2. From managing expertise to exercising judgment** Your team will increasingly use AI to produce analysis, code, models, and recommendations. Your value as a leader isn't knowing everything. It's knowing enough to challenge what you're being shown.

**3. From managing a function to expanding its boundaries** Don't ask: "How can my team do its existing jobs faster?" Ask: "What can my team own now that wasn't economically or technically possible two years ago?"

That is the question I find most exciting. I don't think the data function is disappearing. I think it is becoming much more powerful.

For most of my career, the data organization existed largely to provide the company with information and insight. The AI-native data organization will increasingly build and orchestrate the systems through which the entire company creates intelligence.

That is a very different job. And I think we're only at the beginning.
