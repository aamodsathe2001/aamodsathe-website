In my first two articles, we looked at how the data function is changing from manual production to orchestration, and why managing these highly leveraged teams requires a "kind leadership" style that balances high empathy with an uncompromisingly high technical bar.

Today, I want to go a layer deeper. If our team is no longer just "providing data," and if they are using AI to operate at multiple times their traditional leverage, we have to face a very practical, immediate question:

How do we actually build, organize, and evaluate these teams?

This is not just a data issue. Whether you run a data science team, a software engineering group, a product organization, or a security team, you are likely looking at the exact same puzzle. Traditional career ladders, levels, and job definitions are heavily based on "execution volume"—how much code someone can write, how quickly they can spin up a model, or how many dashboards they can build.

Now, AI handles the baseline tasks in seconds. It generates the SQL, writes the test scripts, and automates the pipeline boilerplate.

So what does "seniority" mean when execution itself is commoditized? How do you define a career path when a junior contributor with a model consensus workflow can produce the volume of output that used to require a mid-level or senior engineer?

If we don't change our organizational design, we are going to end up trying to run 10x operations using legacy career ladders. And that is a recipe for team confusion, mismatched expectations, and organizational bloat.

### The Inflation of Output and the Redefinition of "Junior"

Historically, junior roles (like an L3 or entry-level IC) were heavily task-oriented. You gave them a well-defined specification, and they spent their time writing the code, queries, or ETL pipelines to implement it. They were expected to be "hands" first and "heads" second as they learned the systems.

That tier is effectively disappearing, or at least transforming beyond recognition.

When an entry-level professional can use Claude, Gemini, or specialized internal interfaces to generate the draft of a complex pipeline or write 150 lines of analysis in five minutes, "output volume" ceases to be a useful measure of capability. The bottleneck is no longer how fast they can type syntax.

Because of this, we need to push our expectations for early-career professionals upward. Instead of evaluating them on whether their code runs, we must evaluate them on:

- 
**Output interrogation:** Can they look at the generated analysis and spot the subtle, logical flaw? Can they recognize when the AI has used the wrong table join or made a faulty statistical assumption?

- 
**Problem context:** Do they actually understand why they are running the query, or are they just prompting toward a superficial request?

- 
**System curiosity:** Are they actively studying the schema and architectural boundaries rather than relying blindly on the tool?

This means the gap between "knowing how to build" and "knowing what to build" has shrunk. We can no longer afford to let junior folks spend their first year or two in a task-execution sandbox. They need to develop systems thinking and domain expertise on day one.

### What Does "Senior" Mean Now?

If junior roles are being pushed upward, the definition of a "senior" or "lead" contributor has to evolve just as fast.

Historically, you became senior by mastering a specific technical craft and proving you could solve highly complex execution problems. You were the person who could fix the broken, low-latency pipeline or hunt down a rare, massive scale edge case.

In an AI-native world, those technical skills remain absolutely essential, but they are no longer the ceiling. The value of a senior contributor now lives entirely in what I call the **Architectural and Judgment layers**:

- 
**System & Design Thinking:** AI can write five microservices, but it cannot yet foresee how they will fail when one of them lags under real-world traffic. A senior contributor must have the architectural foresight to challenge structural choices, find fragile integrations, and protect the system's integrity.

- 
**Product Rigor and "The So What?":** The world has plenty of analysis; it has very little actionable product judgment. A senior team member's job is to ruthlessly filter out the noise, connect the dots, and determine if an AI-assisted recommendation is actually solving the user’s problem or if it’s just a "cool" piece of tech.

- 
**Risk and "The 1% Case":** It is relatively easy to generate a model or automated system that works 90% of the time. But in security, data trust, or large-scale consumer applications, the 1% edge cases can wreck a business. Seniority is knowing where those hidden failure modes are and putting up the guardrails to keep them from crashing the plane.

### Collapsing Job Families into Shared Outcomes

As boundaries blur, trying to maintain distinct, hyper-specialized ladders is becoming an exercise in organizational friction.

In my view, we need to move toward broader, outcome-based roles rather than strictly bounded functional silos. In data, the "Full-Loop" IC is becoming a reality:

- 
A **data scientist** should not stop at an experiment recommendation. With AI, they can take on light engineering work to create a proto-system, write the PR to establish the statistical framework, and hand it to Engineering for final architectural approval.

- 
An **analytics engineer** can no longer just generate transformation code. They must think like software engineers, focusing on low-latency data structures and reliable automated pipelines.

Does this mean everyone needs to do everything? Absolutely not. Attempting to force every data scientist into software engineering will only lead to chaos, technical debt, and team frustration. Specialization still matters.

The change is that we are no longer paying for the manual "handoff." AI allows us to bridge the gaps. We should reward people who use that leverage to extend their boundaries and get solutions closer to the finish line, rather than staying safely inside their traditional functional box.

### Rebuilding the Ladder

So, what does this actually look like in practice? If you are a leader looking at your career ladders right now, I believe we need to actively update our leveling rubrics to reflect three key shifts:

- 
**De-emphasize sheer task execution ➔ Prioritize systems and architectural thinking.** Rubrics should not measure how many pipelines, features, or tickets an IC finishes. They should evaluate how well they predict failure modes, audit generated work, and simplify complex systems.

- 
**Assess task volume ➔ Assess leverage and mentoring.** At higher levels, "seniority" should be evaluated by how effectively an IC uses AI-native workflows to multiply the output of those around them, while enforcing standards and keeping quality from dropping.

- 
**Evaluate isolated output ➔ Evaluate domain expertise and "the human-in-the-loop" judgment.** We need to reward those who possess the failure-tested intuition to know where to inspect, when to challenge, and how to verify that AI-generated work is actually correct.

Developing these criteria is a blueprint I am actively iterating on with my teams. There is no off-the-shelf playbook for this transition. It requires us to get into the details, look honestly at how the day-to-day work is being done, and build a high-trust culture that enables autonomy and ownership.
